The buying case: Subaru Impreza Monte-Carlo ’99 TT-02

The Subaru Impreza Monte-Carlo ’99 is for a rally fan who wants to build a recognizable blue-and-gold model and use it on pavement or relatively flat dirt. Its TT-02 platform is understandable, the body is included and the rally-block tires distinguish it from some ordinary touring-body versions. My chief reservation is that the full-size car’s rally reputation can create unrealistic expectations about the model’s ground clearance and basic suspension. This is a small assembly kit, not a miniature promise of World Rally Championship terrain capability.

Build the version instructed for the Impreza, then check the shell and wheel clearance together.

Before assembly, print or open both the chassis instructions and the body supplement. Keep the gold wheels away from the paint preparation area and identify which decals go over curved surfaces. Give the running chassis its control and rotation checks before the finished body is attached. That sequence lets you solve a gearbox or steering mistake without risking the shell you have just decorated, and makes the first complete-car presentation feel earned rather than hurried.

Drivetrain and power: Subaru Impreza Monte-Carlo ’99 TT-02

The TT-02 is shaft-driven four-wheel drive, with front and rear bevel-gear differentials. A center shaft transfers drive between the gearboxes, while the differentials allow the wheels on each axle to turn at different rates in corners. That describes ordinary 4WD touring and mild-rally running. It does not provide a crawler’s axle locks or the control behavior of a rear-drive drift platform, despite the wide variety of body styles sold on TT chassis.

Tamiya lists a 9.58:1 stock ratio for this Subaru. The global page allows other ratios using optional pinions, but that flexibility is not a reason to choose taller gearing before a first run. Assemble both differentials and gearboxes carefully, check rotation and keep the supplied motor as the initial baseline. A binding driveshaft or overtightened wheel attachment can make a car feel slow without any need for a motor upgrade.

The regional controller capability should be separated from the kit’s intended pack choice. Follow the current supplied ESC instructions for voltage, chemistry and low-voltage settings and verify the battery’s physical fit and connector. The US completion table recommends a 7.2 V battery route; the mere fact that a controller can handle another chemistry does not settle the entire installation. For this buyer, spending time on a free-running driveline and sensible gearing is more useful than immediately converting a freshly painted rally model into a high-speed project.

Radio and setup: Subaru Impreza Monte-Carlo ’99 TT-02

The radio and steering servo are purchased separately, even though the regional kit includes a controller. Choose a compatible transmitter/receiver pair and a servo that fits the stock mounts and saver. If you already own electronics, check them together before assembly rather than discovering at the end that the receiver uses a different protocol or the battery connector requires another purchase. Completion planning is particularly helpful when building from remaining regional stock.

Center the servo before attaching the horn and follow the linkage lengths in the chassis instructions. Calibrate the supplied ESC with the chosen radio, then confirm forward, brake and steering direction with the chassis supported. Check the motor-cover and wiring arrangement before putting the body on. A wire trapped between a shell and a moving steering rod is an installation issue, not a mysterious rally handling trait.

RCScrapyard’s exact 58631 archive discusses the TT-02’s beginner construction and bushings. It is independent setup commentary rather than a controlled drive test of this regional A box. We do not adopt its speed, wear or handling assertions as measured results. Its useful purchasing prompt is to think about bearings and assembly choices before closing the gearbox. Establish the stock build and controls first; an optional bearing decision can be sensible without becoming evidence that every standard bushing will immediately fail on your first outing.

Steering and suspension: Subaru Impreza Monte-Carlo ’99 TT-02

The TT-02 uses four-wheel double-wishbone suspension with friction dampers in the standard Impreza kit. Friction dampers do not provide the same fluid-damping adjustment as an oil-shock system. That is a concrete equipment limitation to understand when comparing the kit with a purpose-built rally RTR. It does not make the Subaru unsuitable for every beginner run; it means its value lies partly in the body and build rather than premium suspension hardware.

Assemble the high-clearance configuration described by the body supplement, then compare wheel movement on both sides. Confirm that the shafts remain seated and that nothing binds through steering and suspension travel. The three-piece steering linkage should move freely before the servo is allowed to pull it. Adjust endpoints so that the servo does not fight mechanical stops, and check clearance again with the Subaru shell fitted.

For a first outing, leave the car at its instructed baseline. If you later fit oil dampers or adjustable upper links, record the change and test on the same surface. Altering tires, ride height, damping and steering settings all at once makes the result difficult to interpret. This review does not claim measured bump control from the high-clearance setup. The purchase benefit is a documented configuration intended for the included body, with a simple suspension that a beginner can inspect and understand before choosing a justified upgrade.

Where to drive: Subaru Impreza Monte-Carlo ’99 TT-02

Tamiya identifies pavement and flat dirt as suitable TT-02 territory for this model. The rally-block tires support that mild-rally brief and distinguish it from a kit supplied with racing radials. They do not make deep gravel, long grass or broken stone a suitable first practice area. Look for a relatively smooth route with small, manageable undulations rather than assuming that the Impreza body licenses rough-ground bashing.

I would start on clean pavement to verify tracking and braking, then move to compact dirt once the build checks pass. Notice whether dust accumulation or changing grip makes the car inconsistent, and inspect the running gear between short sessions. A rally-looking circuit made of smooth dirt and gentle corners is a more reasonable goal than copying the jumps of the full-size car. There is no documented model-specific top-speed or runtime result behind our recommendation.

Dust can reach the open chassis even when the gearboxes are enclosed. Remove debris around the battery tray, suspension pivots and shafts, and avoid dragging dirt into the electronics while lifting the shell. A separately sold cover may be useful later, but we do not treat it as included or as proof of waterproofing. Your chosen radio and the actual controller establish their water-use limits. A dry practice area keeps the first driving observations relevant to the chassis instead of turning every outing into a contamination test.

Body and service: Subaru Impreza Monte-Carlo ’99 TT-02

The two-door Impreza silhouette, large rear wing, blue livery and gold wheels are the purchase hook. Tamiya’s polycarbonate body captures that recognizable combination and includes separate molded details. The painting and decal work deserve time. Trim and check the shell before spraying, follow the body supplement’s hole sizes and positions, and use paint intended for polycarbonate on the appropriate surface. A neat finish is a real part of this kit’s value.

The sponsor graphics are extensive. Plan their order around windows, contours and panel edges instead of applying the first attractive sticker without reference to the diagram. Do not attach the mirrors while the shell still needs repeated trial fitting on the bench. Those details are visually useful and physically exposed; our research contains no controlled crash test that would justify calling them durable. A practice area with room away from walls helps preserve the model you have built.

The official body supplement is included in the gallery as a technical illustration, not a photograph of our car. It explains trimming and finishing operations for this exact Subaru body. The stock photograph is manufacturer photography of a completed example. That distinction prevents the evidence from implying we assembled or drove the kit. For service, keep the body removable and ensure the radio wiring stays with the chassis, so routine battery changes do not repeatedly stress the details and decoration.

Buying verdict: Subaru Impreza Monte-Carlo ’99 TT-02

The Impreza is convincing for the Subaru fan who wants a first body-inclusive mild-rally build and accepts that painting is part of the project. Its exact regional controller package, stock rally blocks and documented high-clearance setup support that purpose. The reservations are the remaining electronics and power budget, friction dampers, and discontinued US stock status. Those details deserve the same visibility as the blue-and-gold body.

Our equally weighted categories assess subject presentation, build clarity, supplied equipment, service understanding and complete-purchase value for that named buyer. We do not score measured grip, crash survival, runtime or competitive pace. Manufacturer documents establish the hardware; Amazon establishes the exact listing and offer; RCScrapyard supplies independent contextual discussion. The source distinctions remain visible, including the regional ESC difference and the manufacturer’s discontinued designation.

I would choose 58631-A when the Impreza livery is the thing you want to create and you have pavement or compact dirt available. Follow both manuals, arrange the radio and battery before finishing the shell, and keep the first setup simple. I would pass for deep-ground bashing, immediate RTR driving or a competition venue requiring another car. This model earns its recommendation through a clear build-and-drive role, provided you let the small RC chassis set the terrain expectations instead of the history of the full-size Subaru.
