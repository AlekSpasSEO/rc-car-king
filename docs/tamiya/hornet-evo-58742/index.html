Tamiya kept the Hornet silhouette and rebuilt what happens under it. The 58742 kit is for a builder who wants an old-school buggy with more to set up and learn.

01 /

The difference shows up at the rear wheels

The Hornet EVO makes the most sense if you remember the original shape and want a buggy you can tune at each corner. Tamiya kept the slim plastic tub and recognizable silhouette, but the rear suspension is a different proposition. The original Hornet moves much of the rear assembly together. On the EVO, the left and right rear wheels each move on their own arms and use a dogbone to transmit power. On a rutted bend, a bump under one wheel therefore does not require the other wheel to follow the same suspension movement.

There are two good reasons to buy a Hornet. One is to revisit the engineering of the original; the other is to build something that wears the same shape and gives you more alignment choices. The EVO serves the second. The original has fewer pieces in its rear suspension, which is part of its appeal as a time capsule. Greg Vogel’s RC Driver feature shows 58742 as a complete build and takes it onto dirt. It helps place the hardware in context; it does not give this review an independent set of measured lap times or a universal surface recommendation.

02 /

The kit supplies the buggy, not the finished bench job

The export kit has the clear polycarbonate body and wing, driver figure, decals, wheels, tires and the pieces for the complete chassis. Tamiya lists a brushed 540 motor and Tamiya USA specifies the Hobbywing THW 1060 brushed controller. The global catalog describes the ESC as included for export; the separately required radio wording on that page also mentions an ESC. Use the US package description for this review and check the actual regional box before placing a second controller on the order.

You add the transmitter and receiver, steering servo, matched drive battery and charger, and the batteries specified by your chosen radio. Painting a clear shell adds its own items: polycarbonate paint, cutting tools and the finishing materials for the driver if you want the catalog look. Leave time for masking and paint to cure according to the materials. The official product photographs show the assembled and decorated example. The decals give you the flames and number treatment; the color under those markings is work you supply.

03 /

What the sealed gearbox and 17-tooth pinion buy you

The motor sits at the rear and drives a sealed gearbox based on Tamiya’s DT-02 layout. The gear differential inside allows the outside rear wheel to rotate faster through a turn. A dogbone on each side carries drive to the wheel while that suspension corner travels. The sealed case helps shield the gears from loose dirt but is no promise that the whole chassis keeps dust out. The shafts, drive cups and outboard bearings still live in the area where the rear tires kick up grit.

Tamiya specifies a 17-tooth pinion for a 9.28:1 overall reduction and describes the ratio as fixed in the US specification. I would build it as drawn. The point of this kit is to learn how a stock 540 and the rear tires work together on a small course. Changing tooth count because an original Hornet option happens to fit its motor shaft ignores the EVO gearbox and gear spacing. Likewise, the bearings are already included, so an order for a generic Hornet bearing conversion is a poor way to spend the completion budget.

04 /

Build around the battery hatch and radio space

The battery slides low into the tub and the radio equipment sits above it. This arrangement leaves a useful distinction between changing the pack and opening the body to reach radio gear. Before fastening the electronics permanently, lay out the installed servo, receiver and ESC with the pack out. Check whether you can close the hatch, remove the pack without snagging its leads and reach the radio connections after the body goes on. A compact receiver can be more helpful here than a transmitter with features you will never assign.

For a first build, budget a manual-supported 7.2 V NiMH setup and a charger intended for that pack. Verify case shape, lead routing, connector and the supplied controller’s instructions before purchasing. The broader voltage text on the US page and the controller’s own ratings do not establish that every pack fits the hatch or belongs in this chassis. Set neutral and servo travel per your radio and the 58742 manual; check full steering movement with the wheels clear of the bench and the model supported as instructed. A servo pushing against a stop is a setup problem to solve before dirt makes it harder to spot.

Keep the model number on your bench checklist if you are building from a secondhand box. Compare the supplied gear differential and bearing set with the parts drawings before closing the gearbox. The EVO’s bearings are part of the design, not an optional finish detail; missing or substituted hardware can change how the shafts sit. Photograph the gearbox and upright assemblies at the points where the manual shows the bearings and spacers in order. That small record is useful later when a wheel feels different from its mate and you need to know which parts go behind it.

05 /

Treat the four shocks as pairs and keep the manual baseline

The double wishbone layout uses separate upper and lower links front and rear. The upper links use turnbuckles, giving you a way to set camber without changing arm lengths at random. For a first outing, build to the manual’s link lengths. Check left and right against one another with the buggy on level ground at its running stance. If it leans while stationary, check shock assembly, spring seating and suspension binding before using a turnbuckle to correct a difference that came from somewhere else.

The CVA units are real oil dampers at all four corners. Keep the left and right damper on each axle matched while assembling them, and compare their motion before attaching the springs. A corner that sticks on the bench will not suddenly become predictable on the track. The hardware makes the EVO a better teaching tool for a builder interested in damping and camber. For someone who wants the quickest possible retro assembly, those extra steps are a real cost in time and attention.

06 /

Pick dirt where the front ribs can guide the car

The front and rear tires have different jobs. The narrow ribbed fronts guide a car with no front drive. The wider rear spikes supply traction under power on loose dirt. On a smooth, firm patch with a shallow loose top layer, that pairing gives the buyer an appropriate starting point for learning how much throttle the rear can use after turn-in. On uninterrupted asphalt the spikes serve a different surface than the one they were selected for. I would use clean pavement for short setup checks and save the regular sessions for dirt.

Treat the first set of rear tires as a stock dirt reference. A worn spike changes how power reaches the ground long before it tells you anything about whether the motor needs replacing. Check that the wheel sits true and that the tire bead is even around the assembled rim. With three pieces holding the tire, confirm the hardware is seated before blaming a hopping wheel on damper settings.

The wheel and tire proportions matter when choosing your practice spot. With 73 mm fronts, a strip of overgrown lawn or a dirt path full of exposed roots can make the front end plow before the rear can use its traction. Start on a maintained dirt course or a permitted open area with mostly shallow ruts and enough space to watch the car from the side. This is a rear-drive buggy built to work a line around the surface. Choose a different vehicle if the main activity is making it through grass or over obstacles you cannot avoid.

07 /

Separate classic-looking parts from EVO replacements

The first body fitting is a dry run. Locate the mounting holes, check how the front and rear sit with the wing in place, and use the manual’s trim lines before painting. The three-piece wheels are another reason to read ahead. Keep the left and right hardware sorted, check that the assembled pieces seat evenly, and use the correct hubs and fasteners for the wheels you fit. The kit includes more than one hub option, which is useful, but the existence of an alternate hub does not prove arbitrary old wheels will clear the arms and body.

If you are assembling more than one Tamiya at a time, label this kit’s shocks and links while the parts are still on the bench. The manual identifies the stock damper oil as #400 in its parts listing. That gives you a known reference if you open a damper later. An oil number from another brand is not automatically the same viscosity. You also do not need to buy heavier oil to make a light buggy behave like a heavier one. The assembly check is whether each damper moves cleanly and the pair on that axle feel alike, with the parts installed in their intended positions.

Keep the exploded diagram and the service list for 58742 with the car. Look up the number before ordering a rear shaft, cup, arm or damper part under the word Hornet. When the buggy returns from dirt, inspect the ribbed fronts and the rear tire fit, move the suspension through its travel with power disconnected, and check the low hatch for stones that could stop it closing. If one wheel stops returning freely, find the mechanical restriction before trying thicker oil or a harder spring.

09 /

Three ways to get a different first buggy

Choose the original Tamiya Hornet 58336 if the historical rear assembly is part of the fun. You still have a true build with a familiar body, but it will not teach you the same rear alignment and damper decisions. The Tamiya Fighter NXGEN DT-04 58741 is a different modern entry kit with a molded body and its own running gear. It is worth comparing if you care more about assembling the chassis than spending a separate session trimming and painting a clear Hornet.

The Losi Mini-B Brushless is the departure from the Tamiya projects. It is a much smaller complete RTR buggy with a brushless system, supplied power equipment and no paint step. Choose it for compact routes and quick access to driving, not because a smaller model is a direct substitute for a 251 mm wheelbase kit. The EVO earns its bench space when the assembly sequence is part of what you want to own. If you already know you will skip tuning and never use the finished buggy on dirt, the less involved alternative is a better use of the same afternoon.

10 /

Buy the EVO for the process, then drive it stock

I would buy 58742 for a builder who wants the familiar body and can name a real place to run a narrow 2WD buggy. The supplied bearings, gearbox and dampers make the car a useful project without requiring a separate cart of mechanical improvements. The drawbacks are clear once the box is open: the body is a finishing job, the electronics must fit a narrow layout, and the full startup basket costs more than the vehicle kit suggests.

Start by securing the correct regional package, price the missing equipment, and save the exact manual. Build the standard transmission and dampers, finish the shell, and use a familiar dirt line to establish what the car does on the standard tires. That gives you an answer when you later ask whether it needs an adjustment. If you mainly want a classic shelf piece or a car to unpack and drive, the original Hornet and a small RTR buggy answer those two needs better.

BEFORE YOU ORDER /

The questions worth asking.

Is the Hornet EVO the same kit as the original Hornet?+

No. Kit 58742 retains the basic shape and tub concept but uses independent double wishbones, a different rear gearbox and dogbones. The original is 58336.

Is the body included and painted?+

The clear polycarbonate body, wing and decal treatment are included. The buyer trims and paints the shell; the catalog shows a completed example.

What motor and ESC are supplied?+

Tamiya specifies a 540 brushed motor. Tamiya USA lists the Hobbywing THW 1060 brushed ESC for its kit. Confirm the regional package before ordering.

What battery should a new builder plan for?+

A suitably sized 7.2 V NiMH pack is one documented starting point. Verify the exact tray, connector, controller instructions and matching charger.

Does it need a bearing or shock upgrade before the first run?+

No separate upgrade is required for the documented build. The kit supplies bearings and front and rear CVA oil dampers. Build them to the manual first.
