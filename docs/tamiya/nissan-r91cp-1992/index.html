01 /

The reissue is a model you can build and drive

The R91CP makes sense if the building and painting are part of the purchase. Tamiya supplies a detailed Nissan endurance body and a mechanical layout that belongs to the older Group C family. The front uses sprung kingpins and the rear is a rigid axle mounted on a flexible T-bar with a separate damper. Put those pieces together and the reason to own the kit becomes clear. You get to finish the car and learn how this particular chassis works before its first outing.

The exact item is 47531, the 2026 reissue. Tamiya’s European page distinguishes it from the 58109 original and 84264 reissue. TamiyaClub records the family history and repeats some launch details, including a wheelbase of 280 mm. Use its entry as context and the current manufacturer material for the build. Do not use an old auction photo, a body-only set or a prior edition’s contents to decide what you will get today.

The Nissan shape is the draw, but the route matters. This is a low car with foam tires and a long body at either end. If your only pavement is broken by joints and stones, the build will spend a lot of time waiting for somewhere appropriate to run. A suitable club or a clean paved area gives the kit a much clearer reason to be assembled.

02 /

Inventory the body, chassis and completion equipment separately

Tamiya includes the Group C chassis parts, wheels and sponge tires, the clear polycarbonate Nissan shell and parts for the rear wing and lights. It also supplies markings, masking stickers and a cockpit with a driver figure. The side mirror, intake pod and wiper add detail after the main shell is finished. The supplied RS-540 Sport-Tuned motor is the useful piece of running equipment in the box. The radio, receiver, steering servo and ESC need a separate plan.

The exact manual’s equipment page calls for a standard two-channel system and shows a small receiver and ESC with a standard-sized servo. It also calls for a Tamiya drive pack and compatible charger. The illustrated pack is a fit clue, but it is still worth verifying the actual shape, connector and electrical compatibility with the ESC you choose. The reference budget assumes you buy that equipment separately, even if an individual dealer offers a different completion bundle.

Sort the build into chassis, body and electrical tasks before opening all the hardware. Lay out the manual’s tool list and identify which materials go with the flexible clear parts and which belong to the rigid details. That keeps the cosmetics from blocking a dry fit of the electronics. It also lets you see whether a small radio component you already own can mount without pushing wires into the rear moving assembly.

03 /

The motor and differential share a simple path to the rear axle

The transverse motor drives the spur at the rear, and the spur incorporates the ball differential. There is no front drive hardware in the stock layout. Tamiya Japan lists a 14T pinion and a 5.00:1 stock reduction. That is the published configuration to keep in your setup notes. Unlike a shaft-driven touring car, the drive path is concentrated behind the main tub. This helps make the assembly easy to understand when reading the diagram.

The ball differential lets the rear wheels turn at different speeds through a corner. It is also an assembled friction mechanism, so the build instructions matter for its sequence and adjustment. If the finished axle turns inconsistently or the car has poor drive, revisit the assembly and gear mesh before shopping for another motor. A stiffer setting cannot solve a misplaced part, and the effect of changing gearing depends on the motor and operating conditions.

The supplied Sport-Tuned motor is a meaningful starting point. It makes the chassis completion decision about an appropriate brushed ESC, fit and supported power rather than an immediate motor hunt. Build the intended drivetrain first and get a repeatable baseline. An existing controller from a different vehicle only counts as saved money if its documentation covers the motor and battery you intend to use.

04 /

Do a dry fit before you commit to the wiring route

The steering servo, receiver and controller go into a chassis with a defined battery space and low body above it. I would dry-fit the components against the exact drawings before fixing them in place. Check that the servo horn and linkage can move without brushing wires, and leave enough space to remove the pack without pulling on the connectors. A small receiver can be helpful here, but the servo must fit the mounting arrangement specified in the manual.

The chosen radio and ESC determine the actual binding, calibration and power limits. There is no stock handset profile to carry over from a Tamiya ready-to-run package. Keep the manufacturer’s default assembly and set neutral and steering travel according to the components you install. With the vehicle prepared according to the manual, verify steering direction and clearance before the first run. A link that reaches a physical stop needs attention rather than a larger transmitter endpoint.

Do not base the power system on a shop’s shorthand battery range alone. The kit manual calls for a Tamiya pack and illustrates the physical arrangement. Confirm that the pack, connector and charger fit the actual kit and that the ESC supports their electrical limits. Budget transmitter cells separately if the radio requires them.

05 /

Kingpins up front and a T-bar behind the tub

Tamiya’s composite chassis uses an ABS bathtub, space frames and an FRP plate. The front suspension is independent, with coil springs at the kingpins. The rear rigid axle assembly moves relative to the tub on a 1.5 mm FRP T-bar and uses a CVA oil damper. That is a different arrangement from four independent coilovers. A rear movement issue can involve the plate or pod assembly as well as the visible yellow damper.

The manufacturer describes two front clearance settings and three rear settings. That gives you a way to accommodate a suitable surface without redesigning the chassis. Check that the rear and front move freely in the manual’s baseline configuration before using the alternative positions. Write the selected spacer arrangement in the setup notes. A reading taken at the body nose should not be confused with the chassis reference used when comparing a clearance change.

Wheelbase is the one specification I would recheck before planning a body project. Tamiya Japan and Tamiya Europe list 280 mm, while the English global page lists 270 mm. Both sides agree on the overall 480 × 204 × 109 mm body dimensions. The conflicting wheelbase numbers should not be averaged. Measure the built car between axle centers if exact fit depends on it.

06 /

The foam tires make the surface decision part of the build

Tamiya specifies front foam tires at 28 mm wide and 64 mm in diameter, and rears at 45 mm wide and 68 mm in diameter. These are the intended wheel and tire proportions for the kit. The broader rear tires sit under the shape of the prototype body and carry the driven axle. Avoid buying replacements based only on the scale label. Check width, diameter, mounting, body clearance and the venue’s surface requirements together.

A clean prepared on-road track or smooth dry paved route suits the low car. Cracks, grit and rough transitions can change the session from driving to repeated inspection. Walk the route before running and look at where the nose and tail will travel under compression. A clean line that has room for the front of the car can still catch the rear wing or body edge on a poorly placed barrier when you turn.

Start with the documented tire sizes and clearance. If the car scrapes, identify where it touches before changing more than one setting. The leading body edge, a tire at steering lock and the underside are different contact points. Raising the chassis is a possible response to one problem, but body seating or wheel fit may be the actual issue with another.

07 /

Build the display details around a body you can remove and refit

The factory picture shows a completed painted model. Tamiya supplies clear polycarbonate for the main body and other pieces, plus separate rigid details. The manual lists PS colors for the flexible material and separate plastic paint references. The window masks and the large livery stickers help, but there is still trimming, masking and a sequence to work through. Review the illustrations before painting the individual light pieces or permanently attaching the wing supports.

The cockpit and driver add depth to the large canopy. There are markings for three driver choices, which is a pleasing detail if the replica is part of the project. The nose pieces, mirror and wiper also give you small parts to protect while carrying the body. Tamiya Europe explicitly describes the light cases as having no provision for LEDs. Treat them as part of the scale finish when planning the build budget.

I would confirm the body seats properly and the wheels clear it at the planned ride height before final decoration. A first fit is easier to correct while the surface is still clear. Later, the cleanest way to protect that work is to inspect the mounts and foam tires before a run and remove dirt gently afterward. Use the exact exploded views when ordering a Group C service item.

The kit price leaves several real purchases on the bench

The USD 144.20 reference is the exact Tamiya 47531 kit listing at RC Hobby Pros, checked 9 November 2026. The remainder below is an editorial planning example for a buyer starting without electronics or tools. It sets aside money for the transmitter and receiver, servo, brushed controller, matching drive pack and charger, handset cells, paint and basic tools. The subtotal is USD 447.20 before tax and shipping.

That budget gives each missing function a line. It does not claim that a named electronics combination is approved or in stock. Check the exact body material before buying paint and the manual’s servo and battery fit before choosing electrical equipment. If you already own suitable items, strike out those allowances only after the compatibility check. If you are paying track fees or want display storage, add those to your own total.

The kit includes the motor and wheel sets, which keeps those out of the basic basket. Allow time for the body finish and setup as well as the money. If the first session is the priority, an assembled vehicle can be more useful than an attractive kit that still needs five separate orders before it can move.

09 /

Decide which part of the project you want to keep

The Tamiya BMW M4 GT3 Evo TT-02 keeps the body-building aspect and moves to a shaft-driven 4WD touring layout. It is an alternative for a builder who wants a contemporary GT shape and a different drivetrain to learn. Read that kit’s own electronics list and body guide. The chassis layout and finishing details from the BMW should stay with the BMW.

The Kyosho Fazer Mk2 Toyota Celica GT is a complete on-road package to compare when a classic shape is the draw and you would rather spend less time on assembly. Its purchase checklist is different because the body and chassis arrive together in an assembled car. Confirm the exact pack and charging equipment in its review before comparing the total.

The Kyosho MINI-Z Porsche 911 GT3 RS reduces the scale and moves the session indoors. You still get a detailed rear-drive car, with a supplied transmitter and separate AAA cells. It serves a driver who has access to a miniature indoor track and wants to drive immediately. The Nissan is the better fit if building a 1/10 endurance prototype is the main attraction.

10 /

Buy it for the finished Nissan and the process of getting there

I would shortlist the 47531 if the group of decisions makes sense together: the Daytona appearance, the direct-drive chassis, a place to drive foam tires and a budget for completion. The included motor and detailed assembly reference give that builder a clear starting point. The full value comes when the same owner wants the bench work and the finished car.

If you want the shortest path from box to track, the paint and electronics steps will feel like delay. If the surface is rough, the low body and intended tire package will also ask for compromises. The Nissan is a specific project with a satisfying brief. Choose it when you want to build that brief, and verify the exact wheelbase directly if you intend to interchange bodies.

BEFORE YOU ORDER /

The questions worth asking.

Is the Nissan body prepainted?+

The 47531 supplies the body unpainted. The official gallery shows an assembled and finished example.

Does the kit include the motor and ESC?+

The RS-540 Sport-Tuned brushed motor is included. Plan a separate compatible ESC for this exact package.

Which wheelbase should I use?+

Japan and Europe list 280 mm. The English global page lists 270 mm. Measure the built chassis for any fit-critical project.

Can I drive it on gravel?+

The low body and stock sponge tires are intended for smooth on-road use. Choose a different venue or vehicle for rough ground.

Is this the original 1992 kit?+

Item 47531 is the 2026 reissue. The 1992 original was 58109, and 84264 was the earlier reissue.

What does the startup example include?+

The USD 447.20 example includes the kit plus allowances for radio, receiver, servo, ESC, battery, charger, radio cells, paint and tools.
