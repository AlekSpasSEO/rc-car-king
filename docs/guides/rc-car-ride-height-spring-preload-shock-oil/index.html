RC car ride height: spring preload, spring rate and shock oil

Measure the car with its running weight, then work out whether the issue is where it sits, how far it moves or how fast it moves.

The Garage EditorPublished 07 Nov 2026Updated 07 Nov 20266-minute read

The short answer

Measure ride height on a flat bench with the car carrying its normal running weight. Settle the suspension and measure at the same front and rear points each time. Use the model’s collars or spacers to adjust where it sits. Spring rate governs how much extra force compresses the spring, while shock oil and piston design govern how quickly the suspension moves. Investigate a corner that binds, leaks or settles inconsistently before tuning.

BEFORE YOU START /

Scope and equipment

This guide covers conventional coilover suspension on electric RC cars. The Traxxas Mini Rally VXL 108046-1 and Team Associated RC10B7.1D 90047 illustrate two documented setups. Use your own manual for the measurement points, adjustment method and limits. For the bench check, keep the normal pack secured and electrically disconnected. Follow the exact shock service instructions if a damper needs to come apart.

  • A firm, level surface, a metric ruler or ride-height gauge, and a place to record measurements
  • The exact vehicle manual and any setup sheet for the installed shock configuration
  • The usual wheels, tires, body and securely installed, disconnected drive pack
  • Factory collars or spacers and the specified hand tools if you need to make an adjustment
01 /

Separate resting position from suspension motion

Ride height is the gap between the bench and a specified point on the underside of the loaded chassis. A reading near the front and one near the rear describe its stance. The lowest screw head or guard might matter when crossing a stone. For setup comparisons, pick the manual’s reference points and use them every time. A different spot on a sloping chassis can produce a different answer with the same suspension.

A collar or spacer shifts the spring seat and changes initial spring compression. With a linear spring, another millimeter still takes the same additional force at the spring. Moving the seat can raise the resting chassis and leave less extension travel for the wheels to follow hollows. The car can feel different even with the original springs because the arms now start at another angle and have a different amount of travel available in each direction.

Damping comes from resistance to oil flowing through the piston. More viscous oil generally adds resistance with the same piston, shock and temperature. Piston holes and geometry also influence that movement. If the chassis returns slowly after you push down, look at damping and friction. Once the car has settled, load, spring force and geometry have the larger say in where it sits.

A spring-rate change makes more sense when the stance is already right but the suspension still uses too much or too little travel under a repeatable load. A collar adjustment can restore the original ride height after changing springs, so keep both settings in your notes. Otherwise two cars can have the same bench height yet respond quite differently when they cross the same bump.

Keep the shock mounting holes in your baseline notes. Moving a shock inboard on an arm changes how far the shaft moves for a given amount of wheel travel. That can change both the spring and damping effects felt at the tire. If you move the mount and turn the collar in the same pass, a matching bench height can hide a change in suspension leverage. Leave the mounts alone when your goal is to isolate preload.

02 /

Measure and adjust a repeatable baseline

  1. Load the car as you normally run it

    Switch off and disconnect power as the manual directs. Secure the usual pack for its weight and fit the body and wheels. A missing battery can lift the chassis. A different tire diameter can change the height reading even if you leave the collars alone. Take a photo or write down the original spring seat positions before moving anything.

  2. Settle the suspension and measure both ends

    Use the model’s settling procedure if it has one. For a simple bench comparison, gently compress and release the suspension and let it come to rest. Keep your hand off the chassis as you read the gauge. Record where you measured at the front and rear and repeat the process. If pressing down and gently lifting the chassis produce noticeably different resting heights, investigate friction or contact before averaging the readings.

  3. Inspect uneven corners before leveling the chassis

    Check that the tires are fully seated and compare the collars or spacers across the affected axle. Look for a trapped wire, an unseated spring cup or contact with the body. With power disconnected, compare left and right movement through a comfortable range. A sharp stop or rough spot needs attention. Extra preload can disguise an uneven stance while the underlying restriction remains.

  4. Make a small, reversible adjustment

    Follow the model’s collar or spacer instructions at the axle you want to change. For a symmetric baseline, start with matched adjustments on both sides. Settle the car again and record both heights. Check that the suspension can still compress and extend and that the tires clear the body. If the height target needs extreme collar travel or leaves little extension, revisit the installed springs, load and assembly.

  5. Compare on a familiar patch of terrain

    Reassemble the car and prepare it to run under its manual. Use the same line and a similar approach speed where the original symptom was easy to see. Watch for the chassis touching, the wheels extending between bumps or a second bounce after a landing. If the change does not help, restore the previous baseline before you try another variable.

Use the stock setup for your exact version

The Mini Rally VXL manual lists preload spacers and describes a starting position with the suspension arms slightly above parallel to the ground. It specifies 50W silicone shock oil at both ends from the factory. That arm-position reference gives a starting point on the stock car. If you also record a ruler measurement, write down where you took it so you can repeat it after a service.

The RC10B7.1D 90047 uses threaded collars. Its Bag 9 build specifies 30wt fluid at both ends, with separate front and rear springs and cups. Set and record the loaded stance once the buggy has its actual electronics, tires and battery. A measurement from the incomplete roller would describe a different weight distribution.

Red RC’s launch coverage distinguishes the dirt version, while its detailed hardware list centers on the standard B7.1. Team Associated describes different spring and piston choices for the two kits. Use the 90047 assembly manual for the dirt kit’s build. Comparing the printed oil numbers across the buggy and Rally cannot tell you which has more damping at the wheel because their pistons, shocks and suspension leverage differ.

04 /

Choose the next check by the symptom

Start by separating one deep compression from a loss of height over a string of bumps. The first can point toward starting height and available compression travel. The second makes it useful to watch whether the wheels extend again between hits. Slow recovery can come from damping, friction or a combination. Start with a repeatable observation on the same section of surface. A different approach angle can change how many wheels hit an obstacle at once.

A wet ring that returns after cleaning, a crooked shaft or a sudden difference in how two corners move deserves a service check. The B7.1D manual documents a bleed-screw cap and a specific bleeding sequence. Use the instructions for your installed dampers and keep service changes consistent across each axle. A different cap may need a different assembly method even if the spring fits over both shock bodies.

Write down the symptom in terms you can check again. For example, record that the left rear stays compressed after you release it on the bench, or that both ends touch on the same ledge. “The shocks feel bad” gives you little to compare. A short record of the running setup, measurement points, original collar positions and one repeatable symptom is enough to decide whether the next job is adjustment or repair.

For an otherwise consistent car that rubs a wheel arch, check where it rubs before raising the whole chassis. With power disconnected, check clearance while the wheel is turned and the suspension is gently compressed within its usual travel. If you find contact only at one corner, check body seating and wheel fit there. A height change can move the rubbing point without correcting a body that has slipped out of place.

READY CHECK /

Your suspension baseline is ready when…

  • You have recorded the exact model, installed shocks, tires and running setup
  • Front and rear readings repeat on the same bench
  • The suspension moves freely and the tires clear the body
  • The original collar or spacer positions are recorded
  • A short run compares one change against the same baseline

COMMON QUESTIONS /

Frequently asked questions

Does more preload make a linear RC spring stiffer?+

Preload adds initial compression and changes the resting position. The spring still takes the same additional force for each extra millimeter of compression.

Should I measure ride height with the battery installed?+

Yes. Secure the usual drive pack with power disconnected and fit the other equipment you normally run. That gives you a measurement with the actual running weight.

Will thicker shock oil raise my RC car?+

Thicker oil generally slows shock movement with the same piston and conditions. Once the suspension settles, check preload, load, geometry and friction to explain the resting height.

Should front and rear ride heights match?+

Use the model’s setup references and record both ends separately. The measurement points and the intended chassis stance can call for different values.
